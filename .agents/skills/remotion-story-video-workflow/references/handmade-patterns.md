# Handmade Direction Patterns

These patterns come from a sample story video production process. Use them as ideas, not as fixed rules.

## Good Places to Add Handmade Direction

- Opening hook: a short, darker setup before the main story starts.
- Chapter starts: a dedicated title treatment before the chapter's story content continues.
- A line that reframes the video: large center text, slow drop-in, or a quiet title card.
- Key words or short interpretive phrases: show only the important phrase, not the full narration text.
- Lists spoken one by one: whiteboard, memo card, checklist, or pinned paper.
- Numbers: a large number card, table, stacked items, or visual count-up.
- Before/after solution: left-right comparison with paired reveal.
- Viewer reflection: a short checklist that asks the viewer to think about their own situation.
- Chapter starts: handwritten title or paper card, with a soft brush/chime sound.
- Flashbacks: warmer color, slower motion, photo/paper feeling, but avoid overdoing filters.
- Medical or serious warnings: quieter sound, less playful motion, slower entry.
- Closing message: simple, calm emphasis rather than decoration.

## Visual Patterns

- Chapter title card: chapter number and title enter separately, with a subtle band, underline, or light sweep.
- Selective emphasis text: one key phrase appears in the center, upper corner, or near a meaningful object while the normal subtitle remains short and readable.
- Explanatory overlay: a short helper phrase that clarifies the meaning of the narration, separate from the spoken script.
- Whiteboard overlay: best for "three points" or "what to check".
- Memo card: best for a short explanation or calculation.
- Checklist: best for viewer participation.
- Before/after table: best for practical solutions.
- Soft underline or hand-drawn circle: only when it points to a meaningful object.
- Small guide character: useful for warmth, but keep it secondary and avoid hiding important faces/text.
- Large centered text: useful for emotional or important lines.

## Text Overlay Rules

- Do not turn the whole script into large decorative text.
- Keep normal subtitles separate from emphasis/explanatory overlays.
- If an emphasis overlay repeats the same or nearly the same wording as the normal subtitle, hide the normal subtitle only for that scene to avoid duplicate text.
- If an explanatory overlay adds different meaning from the normal subtitle, both may remain visible, but only when they do not overlap or compete.
- Prefer short phrases: usually 1-12 Japanese characters for emphasis, or one short sentence for explanation.
- Vary placement across scenes: center, top-left, top-right, side, or just above the lower subtitle area.
- Vary treatment gently: size, color, opacity, delay, fade, underline, or one-word reveal.
- Keep enough contrast for readability, but avoid covering important faces, animals, hands, or props.

## Sound Patterns

- Paper or pen tick: for list items appearing.
- Soft chime: for gentle emphasis.
- Brush pop: for chapter cards or handwritten title changes.
- Water ambience: for faucet or kitchen scenes.
- Morning birds: for calm morning kitchen scenes.
- Kitchen room tone: for quiet cooking scenes.

Avoid using sounds that feel like drums, gongs, or heavy alarms unless the story is intentionally dramatic.

## Video AI Candidates

Good candidates for image-to-video:

- subtle hand tremor
- chopsticks or spoon hesitating
- steam rising from miso soup
- faucet water continuing to run
- salt grains slowly accumulating
- hose tightening under pressure
- balloon slowly inflating
- blood pressure monitor tension
- warm memory of mother and miso soup
- careful dashi preparation
- healthy breakfast gradually feeling suspicious

Usually keep these in Remotion:

- numeric explanations
- official target values
- comparison tables
- checklists
- chapter titles
- citations
- Japanese text overlays

## Practical Warnings

- If a sticky note or memo is too small, increase it rather than adding more decoration.
- Remove lines or circles that do not clearly guide attention.
- If a scene feels lonely, add a small reaction, sound, or paper element before adding many objects.
- If a video clip is slightly short, slowing it down is often safer than regenerating it.
- If generated video distorts important objects, return to the original image or use a safer prompt.
